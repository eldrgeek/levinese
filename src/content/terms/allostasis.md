The process by which a biological system achieves stability through dynamic adjustment of its setpoints in response to anticipated future demands, as distinct from homeostasis (which maintains a fixed setpoint). Where homeostasis asks "am I at the target?" allostasis asks "given what I expect, what should my target be?" A cell or tissue operating allostatically does not merely correct deviations from a fixed goal — it adjusts the goal itself in response to predicted load. In Levin's framework, this distinction becomes clinically significant when designing morphoceuticals: a therapy that resets a homeostatic setpoint is doing something different from one that restores allostatic flexibility. Aging, on this view, may involve losing not just the morphostatic "memory" of correct form, but also the allostatic capacity to dynamically recalibrate that memory in response to changing conditions.
